Issue 16¾ August 18, 2026 Field Bulletin

Gilded Trees, Standing Among Stars

A special dispatch about the real ocean, which has once again out-written us: scientists have described golden, tree-like corals over a metre tall growing on deep Pacific seamounts — so unlike anything known that they were handed a new species, a new genus, and a whole new family. As of this afternoon, they grow in the reef you play, under their real name.

The dispatch has never published three issues in one day, and the masthead's numbering system — which already strained itself inventing half-issues — was not designed for it. But some news does not wait for Tuesday, and this news is four hundred million years old and one publication young: there are golden trees in the deep Pacific, and until very recently, nobody with a surname knew.

What was found

On rocky seamounts off Costa Rica — near Isla del Coco and along the Pacific margin — at depths of 360 to 529 metres, researchers found colonies of a gorgonian octocoral unlike anything in the books: bright yellow, tree-shaped, standing over a metre tall in the dark. The colonies were photographed and collected by the remotely operated vehicle SuBastian, on expeditions run by the Schmidt Ocean Institute in 2019 and 2023, and the years between collection and publication were spent doing the slow, careful work of proving the unbelievable: this coral fit no existing species. Then no existing genus. Then no existing family. It has all three now, newly minted: the species Laurinque elenya, the genus Laurinque, the family Laurinqueidae — a brand-new branch grafted onto the tree of life, made of trees.

And the detail we cannot stop thinking about: the rocks the golden trees grow from are carpeted with thousands of brittle stars. The researchers described the scene as a magical spectacle — gilded trees, standing among stars. That is not our purple prose. That is what it looks like, five hundred metres down, where no light reaches and the forest is its own gold.

A new family is not a new fish in a known drawer. It is the discovery that the cabinet has a drawer nobody had opened — and that inside it, there was a forest.

What it means, aged nine and up

Taxonomy has ranks, and the rank matters. A new species is a good day at the office. A new genus is a career highlight. A new family — Laurinqueidae now stands beside families that contain hundreds of species each — means the ocean has been quietly running an entire lineage, possibly for millions of years, without appearing in a single field guide. It means the map has provinces missing. Most of the deep sea has never been visited by anything with a camera; discoveries like this are not the last crumbs of an explored world but the first proofs of an unexplored one. Somewhere down there, right now, something else tall and golden — or short and impossible — is waiting out its last unnamed years.

The reef responds, within hours

Our publisher read about the golden trees a few days ago and, this afternoon, commissioned them with characteristic brevity: "add Golden Trees." The reef obliged. The Golden Tree now stands in the 3D reef's pearl catalogue — a driftwood trunk crowned in golden tufts, forty pearls, no income, no function, pure light. It glows after dark, it casts a real pool of lamplight onto the sand, and in defiance of strict biogeography it plants in any biome, because we run a reef, not a customs office. Its Ocean Journal entry carries the true story — the depths, the brittle stars, the new family — under its true name, Laurinque elenya, so that anyone who taps the entry learns something the field guides themselves only just learned.

A correction, in the family tradition

Transparency compels us to report that for approximately twelve minutes this afternoon, the Golden Tree was published under the invented scientific name Arbor aurea, because the editorial staff built the tree without recognizing the commission's source material. The publisher caught it. The record was corrected. Arbor aurea joins the Fourth of July coverage, the till-less shop, and the original biography of Gavin in our growing archive of things we said confidently before checking — an archive which, we note with some discomfort, is becoming one of this publication's most consistent sections. The real name is better anyway. Most real things are.

— The Reef Bloom team, with gratitude to the crews of the SuBastian
